Betting Guide

Everything you need to understand sports betting — how odds work, what the model uses, and what we honestly can and can't track.

Core Concepts & Glossary
Point Spread
The handicap given to the favorite to level the playing field. The favorite must win by more than the spread; the underdog can lose by less than the spread and still cover.
Example: Alabama -7.5 means Alabama must win by 8 or more for a spread bet on them to win. If you bet the underdog (+7.5), they can lose by up to 7 points and you still win your bet.
Moneyline (ML)
Bet on who wins straight up — no spread involved. The odds reflect implied probability. Favorites are negative (you risk more to win less); underdogs are positive (risk less to win more).
Example: -200 means bet $200 to win $100. +175 means bet $100 to win $175.
Over/Under (Total)
A bet on the combined score of both teams in a game. The sportsbook sets a total; you bet whether the actual combined score goes over or under that number.
Example: If the total is 52.5 and you bet OVER, both teams must score 53 or more combined points for you to win.
Parlay
Combining 2 or more bets into a single wager. All individual "legs" must win for the parlay to pay out. Higher risk — but the reward multiplies with each added leg.
Example: A 4-team parlay where 3 teams win and 1 loses is a losing bet. Missing even one leg kills the whole parlay.
Juice / Vig
The sportsbook's commission on every bet. It's built into the odds so books profit regardless of outcome. Standard juice is -110 on both sides of a spread bet.
Example: At -110, bet $110 to win $100. The extra $10 is the vig — that's how the house stays profitable long-term.
ATS (Against The Spread)
A measure of how often a team covers the point spread, as opposed to simply winning or losing the game outright. A team's ATS record can differ significantly from their win/loss record.
Example: A team can win the game 28-20 but lose ATS if they were favored by 10 — they only won by 8, so they didn't cover.
Push
When the final margin exactly equals the spread. Neither side wins nor loses — your original bet is refunded in full. This is why lines often use half-points (e.g., -7.5) to eliminate pushes.
Example: If the spread is -7 and the favorite wins by exactly 7, the bet pushes and everyone gets their money back.
Line Movement
When the point spread or total changes from when it was first posted to when the game kicks off. Lines move in response to betting volume, sharp money, and new information like injuries.
Example: A line opening at -3 and moving to -6 means heavy action came in on the favorite. If it moves against public betting percentages, that's a sharp signal.
Sharp Money
Bets placed by professional bettors or betting syndicates who have enough volume and accuracy to force sportsbooks to adjust their lines. Sharp money is considered the most reliable signal in betting markets.
Example: 80% of public bets are on Team A, but the line moves toward Team B — that reverse line movement indicates sharp money is on Team B.
Public Money
Where the majority of casual bettors are placing their money. Sportsbooks track public betting percentages. The public tends to overvalue favorites, home teams, and brand-name programs.
Example: If 75% of bets are on a big-name favorite, the book may shade the line to attract action on the other side and balance their risk.
Opening Line / Closing Line
The opening line is the spread or total when first posted, often days before the game. The closing line is the final spread at kickoff. Closing lines incorporate all available information and are considered the most accurate.
Example: Getting -3 on a team that closes at -6 means you "beat the closing line" — a sign of good timing or information.
Cover
When a team beats the point spread. If a team is favored by 7 and wins by 10, they covered. If an underdog is +10 and loses by 6, they covered.
Example: "Alabama covered" means they won by more than the spread required — bettors who backed Alabama against the spread won their bet.
Dog
Short for underdog. The team expected to lose, indicated by a + spread and + moneyline. Betting the dog pays out more because they're less likely to win or cover.
Example: Team B at +7.5 spread and +220 moneyline is the dog. A $100 moneyline bet pays $220 profit if they pull the upset.
Chalk
The favorite. Betting chalk means betting the expected winner. Chalk has a - spread and - moneyline. "Parlaying chalk" means stacking multiple heavy favorites together.
Example: Georgia at -14 and -650 moneyline is chalk. You'd need to bet $650 to win $100 on the moneyline.
Steam
A sudden, coordinated rush of sharp money on one side that causes rapid line movement across multiple sportsbooks simultaneously. Steam moves are usually orchestrated by professional betting groups.
Example: A line drops from -6.5 to -4.5 within minutes at books across the country — that's a steam move, and it's a significant sharp signal to track.
7 Signals — Weighted Prediction Engine
30%
Base Model
SP+ ratings from ESPN / collegefootballdata.com — a predictive efficiency metric that adjusts for strength of schedule, tempo, and garbage time. It measures how efficiently a team scores and prevents scoring on a per-play basis. Higher SP+ = better team. This is the single most reliable predictor of game outcomes and forms the backbone of every projection.
20%
Player Impact
Key player grades for stars who can meaningfully shift a game's outcome, injury status pulled from ESPN, and starter vs. backup situations at quarterback and other skill positions. A backup QB can swing a projection by multiple points. Injury reports are the most time-sensitive signal in the model.
15%
Situational
Historical ATS records in specific game situations: coming off a blowout loss, facing ranked opponents, night games, playing far from home, rivalry matchups, bowl games, and conference championship contexts. Teams behave differently in different situations — this signal captures those tendencies.
10%
Weather
Wind speed, temperature, and precipitation at game time. High wind (15+ mph) suppresses passing offenses and benefits run-heavy teams — it also strongly favors the under. Cold weather disproportionately affects teams that train and play in warm climates. Rain reduces ball security and field goal percentage.
10%
Coaching Edge
A coach's career ATS record split by role (favorite vs. underdog), late-game decision-making tendencies, timeout management track record, and historical performance in big spots. Some coaches consistently beat the spread as underdogs; others lose ATS as heavy favorites. This captures that edge.
8%
Program Momentum
Transfer portal wins and losses (did the team add talent or lose key contributors?), recruiting class trajectory, locker room stability signals, and fan morale indicators. A program that lost 4 starters to the portal and had coaching staff turnover is not the same team as last year's SP+ suggests.
7%
Sharp Money
Line movement from open to current spread, detection of reverse line movement (line moving against public betting percentages), and steam signals. When sharps disagree with the public and the line moves their way, it's a meaningful signal — though it's weighted lowest because by the time data is available, some edge has already been priced in.

When The Model Struggles — Honest Limitations
BE AWARE: These scenarios have historically reduced model accuracy
First-year head coaches — No track record means no reliable ATS data. Year-1 coaches routinely over- or under-perform vs. historical team-level projections. Treat high-confidence picks on teams with new HCs with extra skepticism.
Extreme late-breaking injuries (≤48h before kickoff) — The model reacts to known injuries but if a starting QB goes down after our analysis publishes, the pick may be outdated. Always check the intel page before betting.
Week 1 (pre-season data only) — No in-season results to calibrate on. All projections rely on prior-year SP+ and recruiting data. Accuracy historically improves 15-20% after Week 4 once real game data flows in.
Major rivalry games (spreads 10+) — Rivalry motivation systematically compresses actual margins. The model accounts for this via rivalry flags but very large spreads in rivalry games are historically soft — underdogs cover at ~58% rate.
Teams with massive portal turnover (10+ transfers in/out) — Chemistry and scheme integration effects are hard to quantify. The model uses portal ratings but heavy roster churn adds noise beyond what the numbers capture.
Key numbers (3, 7, 10, 14) — When the line sits on or near a key number, spreads are inherently harder to predict. The model flags these but they represent genuine uncertainty in expected margin.
5 Rules for Smart Bankroll Management
1
Never bet more than 2–3% of your bankroll on any single game
Even the best sports bettors in the world win at ~58–60% — which means they lose 40–42% of the time. A bad weekend or a 5-game losing streak is statistically normal. Betting 10% per game can wipe out your bankroll in a single bad week; betting 2% gives you 50 bets before you run dry, enough to survive variance and let edge play out.
1 unit = 1% of your bankroll. Our max is 2u (ELITE picks). That's 2% per game — never more.
2
Match your bet size to confidence — every tier means something
Flat betting (same amount every game regardless of confidence) ignores the most valuable signal you have: the model's own uncertainty. ELITE picks have historically hit at ~89% — they deserve twice the size of a MEDIUM pick. LOW picks have noisy signals; if you bet them at full size, you erode the edge that high-confidence picks build.
Use the Bankroll Calculator on the home page — enter your bankroll and see exact dollar amounts for each game automatically.
3
Set a weekly stop-loss — and actually follow it
Decide before the week starts: if you lose X amount, you stop betting for that week. A reasonable number is 5–6% of your total bankroll (roughly 3 max-size bets). Chasing losses is the single biggest bankroll killer. The model's edge comes from volume over time — not from doubling down after a bad Saturday to "get it back."
Tilt is real. A stop-loss removes the decision from emotion. Write the number down before Week 1.
4
Track every bet — wins and losses, not just the wins
You cannot know if you have an edge without tracking. Most recreational bettors only remember their wins. Keep a spreadsheet: date, game, pick, odds, units risked, result. After 50 bets, you'll have real data. Are you hitting at 54%+? Are your ELITE bets actually performing better than your LOW bets? If the data says no, adjust.
The Record page tracks the model's picks. Use the "My Picks" page to log your own bets alongside them.
5
Resize your units when your bankroll changes significantly
If your bankroll grows 25%, your unit size should grow with it. If you take a bad run down 20%, reduce your unit size to the new number. This is called proportional (Kelly-adjacent) betting — it protects against ruin on the way down and compounds your edge on the way up. Don't stay at your original unit size after a major swing in either direction.
Rule of thumb: recalculate your unit at the start of each month or after any 15%+ bankroll swing.

The Kelly Criterion — In Plain English
The Kelly Criterion says: bet the fraction of your bankroll proportional to your edge. If you have a 5% edge on a -110 bet, Kelly says bet ~5% of your roll. Our unit sizing is a fractional Kelly (we use 50% of full Kelly) — this preserves bankroll during bad variance while still growing it when the edge holds. Pure Kelly is too aggressive for most bettors; our system captures most of the upside with far less ruin risk.

Not every game is worth betting. Here are the conditions that should increase or decrease your conviction beyond what the model says.

How to Read Line Movement

The line moves for a reason — and understanding why tells you who's betting what. Here are the 4 movement patterns worth knowing.

SHARP
Reverse Line Movement (RLM) — Most Valuable Signal
Public betting is 65%+ on Team A, but the line moves toward Team B. This means the smaller percentage of bettors on Team B are betting large enough to force the book to move. Those bettors are almost always professionals. This is the strongest indicator of sharp money. When RLM aligns with our model pick, consider it a confirmation signal.
PUBLIC
Line Moves With Public — Typical, Adds No Information
80% of bets on Team A and the line moves toward Team A. This is just the book balancing action. It tells you where casual money went — which is mildly useful for contrarian plays but isn't a sharp signal. Don't over-read this as confirmation of a pick.
INJURY
Sudden Large Move (2.5+ pts) — Usually Injury News
When a line jumps 2.5 or more points quickly without an obvious reason, check the injury report immediately. A starting QB or All-American going down can cause an instant book adjustment of 3–7 points. If you already have a bet placed and the news affects your team, it's worth reconsidering the position.
STEAM
Steam Move — Coordinated Sharp Action
A line moves 2+ points in the same direction at multiple sportsbooks within minutes of each other. This is a professional betting syndicate hitting multiple books simultaneously to get money down before the market adjusts. Steam moves are rare but powerful signals — when they happen, the direction of the move is the sharpest information available that day.
Parlay Strategy — When It Makes Sense

Parlays pay more but lose more. Here's how to use them without destroying your bankroll.

DO
2–3 leg parlays anchored by ELITE or HIGH picks
A 2-leg parlay of two ELITE picks (~89% each) has roughly a 79% hit rate — which at +260 typical payout is solidly +EV. The math works when the individual legs are strong. Keep total parlay risk to 0.5–1u regardless of confidence — the variance is still higher than single bets.
DO
Mix spread and total picks when uncorrelated
An ELITE spread pick (Team A covers -7) combined with a total UNDER from a different game are uncorrelated — if one loses it tells you nothing about the other. Correlated parlays (Team A covers AND the game goes over) are worse — they tend to fail together because the same event (high-scoring game) helps or hurts both.
AVOID
5+ leg parlays as a regular strategy
At 55% per leg (a great hit rate), a 5-leg parlay hits only 5% of the time. The expected value is almost always negative after accounting for the vig built into each leg. Sportsbooks make most of their profit on big parlays. Treat 5+ leg parlays as entertainment, not strategy — maximum 0.25u.
AVOID
Same-team parlays to "hedge" a large bet
Parlaying a team to cover AND win on the moneyline is correlated — they'll both win or both lose together. You're not adding legs with independent value; you're adding legs with no additional edge but additional vig baked in. The sportsbook loves this play — you should avoid it.

7 Mistakes That Kill Bankrolls
1. Chasing losses with bigger bets
You lost 3 games Saturday. The solution is NOT to double your Sunday bets to "get it back." Losses happen to every bettor — even sharps. Stick to your unit sizes regardless of recent results. The edge is in the math over time, not in any single day.
2. Parlaying 5+ games because the payout looks exciting
A 10-leg parlay at +5000 sounds incredible. But at 55% per leg, it hits 0.25% of the time. At 50% per leg, it hits 0.097% of the time. The sportsbook margin on these bets is enormous. Occasional 2–3 leg parlays on your best picks — that's the sustainable version.
3. Ignoring line movement before you bet
The model found value at -3. If the line is now -6 when you check it, the edge is gone — and possibly flipped. Always check current lines, not the lines from when the pick was made. Check the game page's line movement section for context on how the spread has moved.
4. Betting every game on the card
There are 21 games in our system. Not all 21 are worth betting. The model flags PASS games for a reason — they have noisy signals and the expected edge is negative or minimal. Selective betting is the most underrated skill in sports betting. 6 strong bets beat 15 average bets every single week.
5. Over-weighting recent results ("hot streak" thinking)
Won 4 in a row? Don't increase unit sizes as a reward — that's correlation, not causation. Lost 3 straight? Don't reduce sizes out of fear — that's when the model's historical edge is most likely to reassert. Recency bias destroys bankroll management discipline. Resize units based on your total bankroll level, not last week's results.
6. Betting teams you "know" without checking the model
You watched every Alabama game this year — you're not betting against them no matter what the numbers say. This is emotional betting disguised as expertise. Your subjective view of a team is already priced into the line by millions of other bettors who also watched them. The model accounts for this and sometimes correctly fades popular teams.
7. Not reading the Intel page before placing big bets
The model runs before the final injury reports drop. If a key player's status changes in the last 24 hours, the pick may no longer reflect the best information. Always check the Intel page and the game's injury section before betting more than 1u. Last-minute news is where the edges and the traps both live.
The Bottom Line
Long-term profit in sports betting comes from one thing: consistently finding bets where your estimated probability is higher than the implied probability in the odds. Our model does the heavy lifting — your job is to manage your bankroll so you're still alive when the edge plays out. No bankroll management system can turn a 48% bettor into a winner. But terrible bankroll management can turn a 56% bettor into a loser.
